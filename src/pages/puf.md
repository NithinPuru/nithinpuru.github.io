## Abstract

<!-- BEGIN abstract -->
***Abstract*-Floating multimodal electrochemical sensors report the glucose concentration and cell density inside bioreactors to the process control loop, readings that an adversary may seek to intercept or forge. The secure delta-sigma modulator (S-DSM) of Yasar *et al.* [1] protects such readings inside the analog-to-digital converter: each 1-bit modulator output is XORed with the keystream of a 16-bit linear-feedback shift register (LFSR), for a reported 1.8 % power overhead and up to 2.67× lower wireless energy than a decimate-then-encrypt design. We propose deriving the key from a physically unclonable function (PUF), so that no key is held in on-chip non-volatile memory (NVM): a 128-bit key is regenerated at every power-up from the start-up state of 600 static random-access memory (SRAM) cells, corrected by a fuzzy extractor with a Bose-Chaudhuri-Hocquenghem (BCH) code and hashed, and is held only in volatile registers while the chip is powered. The key drives the Grain-128a stream cipher, which also authenticates every frame: it computes over the frame a 32-bit message authentication code (MAC), a tag that only the key holder can produce, so the receiver rejects an altered or forged frame except with probability 2<sup>−32</sup> per attempt. Each frame also carries a frame counter, so that a replayed old frame is rejected, and the cipher is initialised from two nonces, that is, values never repeated under the same key: a session nonce sent by the receiver at the start of each session and a chip nonce derived from the chip's noisy power-up state, so that the keystream is not reused as long as either nonce is fresh. The analog front end and the bit-level XOR of [1] are unchanged. To evaluate both designs, this notebook builds a bit-exact model of the link and checks it before use for consistency with the measured bit-flip probabilities and energy table of [1] (48.2 % and 50.0 % of bits differ against 49.8 % and 50.05 % measured; the ratios of transmitted bits match the energy ratios of [1] within 0.18 %). The principal result is the security evaluation: of 15 attacks, the original design fails 11 in the model; with a simplified representation of the dither of [1], it withstands the cryptanalyses of [1] only as [1] carries them out, and fails once its 16-bit key is brute-forced, a few keystream bits leak, a session nonce is replayed, or the attacker holds the chip or transmits on the link (six of the 11, beyond the eavesdropper evaluated in [1]); in the same model the proposal withstands all 15 (9 verdicts simulated, 2 analytic, 4 assumed). At block level, the model's circuit parameters are then taken from the SkyWater SKY130 open process design kit: the noise and offset of the modulator's StrongARM comparator and the power-up statistics of a six-transistor (6T) SRAM cell, simulated in ngspice from extracted layouts (post-layout, tt: comparator noise 0.58 mV and offset σ 4.4 mV at 27 °C; PUF noise-to-mismatch ratio 0.0225 at 25 °C). Sized from the simulated error rates of 1,000 Monte Carlo chips (a cell model fitted to the post-layout SPICE Monte Carlo of the cell), no BCH code for 600 cells enrolled at 25 °C meets both the 10⁻⁶ key-failure target from 0 to 85 °C, which needs t ≥ 39 at the 99th percentile, and the secrecy budget of a 128-bit key, which allows at most t = 35; an enrolment at two temperatures resolves this (below); the system simulations use t = 45 (94–137 secret bits) as a working setting. At system level, across 45 process, voltage and temperature (PVT) conditions, each effect is first predicted by a first-order theory and then simulated: the simulated sensor (behavioural model with the SKY130 parameters) keeps a signal-to-noise-and-distortion ratio (SNDR) of 60.3–60.4 dB with a margin of about 90× in comparator noise and needs only about 40 dB of integrator amplifier gain; against an attacker who scans the whole capture, with the model's dither, comparator noise protects the original design only from 197× its largest PVT value, at a cost of 7.5 dB of SNDR, and neither comparator offset nor integrator capacitor mismatch protects it at any value tested; and at the fast-nMOS corners some simulated SRAM cells reverse their preferred state with temperature, so the key generator must be sized and enrolled for its worst corner; an enrolment at −40 and 85 °C meets the 10⁻⁶ target at every corner for at least 99.7 % of the Monte Carlo chips with t = 17 and 284–352 secret bits. Yosys synthesis of register-transfer-level (RTL) descriptions, verified against published test vectors, onto the sky130_fd_sc_hd cell library, with every design given the same low-power techniques (a radix-16 parallel state update and clock gating), gives 869, 2,931 and 9,869 gate equivalents (GE) for the S-DSM, the Trivium stream cipher and the proposal's keystream block. The block is placed and routed with OpenROAD (no design-rule violations, layout matching the netlist, timing met at 10 MHz with extracted wires; 16 antenna violations remain), and gate-level simulation under the same conditions for every design gives 64.3 µW for the placed block at 1.8 V and 10 MHz (wire loads estimated on the placement; 73.4 µW with the extracted wires), 6.5× less than as written for one bit per clock and 3.6× the S-DSM in the same flow, a ratio taken within one process, so no process scaling enters it. Without scaling any number between processes, a figure of merit built only from reported results and published process facts, the energy per bit over L<sub>min</sub>V<sub>nom</sub><sup>2</sup> of each design's own process, per bit of security level and per security property provided, gives 29.1 aJ/(nm·V²) for the proposal (gate-level simulation) against 1,603 for the S-DSM measured in [1]. For the same information at an oversampling ratio of 50, the proposal sends 1.44–1.60× fewer bits than the decimate-then-Trivium design of [1], but 3.1× more than a design that decimates on the sensor and sends 16-bit words; no radio is designed, so no radio energy is claimed. A key-generator compiler turns the reliability and secrecy specification into a generated decoder, verified in RTL simulation bit for bit against the model, synthesised and placed and routed: with a 25 °C enrolment no code meets the target at all five corners, while an enrolment at 25 and 85 °C with a repetition-3 and BCH t = 4 code meets it with 1,395 cells and 194 secret bits at the 95 % lower bound of the min-entropy, the same 192-bit budget; with a synthesised SHA-256 core, the designed parts of the key generator and keystream block total 38,638 GE. Finally, a mixed-level simulation of the complete sensor, with the SRAM array and the comparator at transistor level and the key decoder and cipher in RTL, regenerates the key from transistor-level cells and finds the comparator model in agreement decision by decision over 2,000 decisions at tt and 37 °C. Every result is computed by running this notebook in one Colab session or shown from a recorded run of the same code, which a switch re-runs.**

## Key contributions and principal results

**Principal result.** Against 15 attacks, each verdict labelled simulated, analytic or assumed, **the original design of [1] fails 11 and the proposal withstands all 15** in the model (Section IX, Fig. 17). Six of the 11 need an attacker who holds the chip or transmits on the link, beyond the eavesdropper evaluated in [1]; the others break the 16-bit key by brute force, recover the keystream from a few leaked bits, or exploit a replayed session nonce, and the cryptanalyses of [1] succeed once the attacker scans the whole capture (with a simplified representation of the dither of [1]). The proposal's verdicts are nine simulated, two analytic and four resting on stated assumptions, and it detects and rejects five of the attacks.

The contributions as they stand in the published run, in the order in which the notebook establishes them:

1. **PUF-keyed, authenticated in-modulator encryption** (Section IV, Fig. 1). No key is held in on-chip non-volatile memory: a 128-bit key is regenerated at every power-up from SRAM-PUF cells through a BCH fuzzy extractor and a hash, and drives Grain-128a [6] with its MAC, a frame counter, a session nonce and a chip nonce. The analog front end, the modulator and the bit-level XOR of [1] are unchanged.
2. **A bit-exact model, checked against the published measurements of [1] before use** (Sections V and VI). Grain-128a reproduces all 22 published test-vector values; the two-channel test of [1] gives 50.0 % and 48.2 % of bits that differ (50.05 % and 49.8 % measured) and the ratios of transmitted bits match the energy ratios of its Fig. 4(b) within 0.18 % (no radio number used). With its simplified dither, the model reaches the measured value with a small offset between the channels; [1] attributes the disagreement to its dither, which the model does not contradict.
3. **Post-layout SKY130 comparator and SRAM cell** (Section VII). A StrongARM comparator and a 6T SRAM-PUF cell are drawn, DRC- and LVS-clean, extracted and simulated in ngspice (tt, 27 °C, 1.8 V): σ<sub>n</sub> = 0.575 mV, σ<sub>os</sub> = 4.37 mV and 199 fJ per decision; noise-to-mismatch ratio s<sub>25</sub> = 0.0225 and bit-error rates of 1.37, 0.99 and 3.14 % at −40, 25 and 85 °C (recorded Monte Carlo, 1,024 cells).
4. **45 PVT conditions** (Sections VII-F and VIII). The comparator spans σ<sub>n</sub> 0.37–0.81 mV, σ<sub>os</sub> 4.01–5.85 mV and 144–822 fJ, and the simulated sensor (behavioural model with these SKY130 parameters) keeps 60.3–60.4 dB SNDR at every condition. At the fast-nMOS corners the SRAM cell's mismatch rotates with temperature (up to 50° at fs), and 25.6 % of the cells at fs reverse their preferred state at 85 °C; a recorded mechanism check attributes these flips to the latch, not to the testbench.
5. **Full-scan attacker statistics with Clopper–Pearson intervals** (Section VIII). An attacker who scans every 64-bit window of four frames breaks 360 of 360 simulated chip pairs of the original design at the 45 PVT conditions. Comparator noise stops it (0 of 100 pairs; at most 3.6 % per pair at 95 % confidence) only from σ<sub>n</sub> = 160 mV, 197× its largest PVT value, at a cost of 7.5 dB of SNDR; comparator offset, integrator noise and capacitor mismatch never do within their sweeps.
6. **Key sizing with a bounded secret budget** (Sections VII-E and VIII-F). With 600 cells enrolled at 25 °C, no BCH code meets both a key-failure probability below 10<sup>−6</sup> over 0–85 °C and the 192 secret bits that a 128-bit key with a hash margin requires: t = 45 is the working setting of the system simulations and leaves 94–137 secret bits. An enrolment at −40 and 85 °C meets 10<sup>−6</sup> for at least 99.7 % of the simulated chips at every corner with t = 17, leaving 284–352 secret bits (935 cells for 600 kept cells).
7. **A 15-attack evaluation with an atlas** (Section IX). In the model, the original S-DSM fails 11 of the 15 attacks; the proposal withstands all 15 (9 verdicts simulated, 2 analytic, 4 assumed) and detects and rejects 5 of them. One atlas figure draws all 30 verdicts.
8. **A low-power keystream block, compared under the same conditions** (Section X). Every compared design is given the same low-power techniques (radix-16 parallel state update, clock gating) and simulated at gate level under the same conditions. The proposal's block: 9,869 GE on `sky130_fd_sc_hd`, placed and routed on 399 × 399 µm with 0 DRC violations, LVS match, OpenRCX extraction and static timing at three corners (setup slack +85.5 ns at ss, hold slack +0.12 ns at ff); 16 antenna violations remain. In gate-level simulation it dissipates 64.3 µW at 10 MHz (73.4 µW routed), 6.5× less than as written for one bit per clock and 3.6× the S-DSM in the same form. A figure of merit, FOM = E<sub>bit</sub>/(L<sub>min</sub> V<sub>nom</sub><sup>2</sup> S K), compares designs across processes without scaling any number or using any modelled one: 29.1 aJ/(nm·V²) for the proposal against 1,603 for [1]'s measured S-DSM.
9. **A key-generator compiler** (Section XI). From a reliability and secrecy specification to a generated decoder, verified in RTL simulation, synthesised and routed: a 25 °C enrolment cannot meet 10<sup>−6</sup> at all five corners with any code searched; a two-temperature enrolment can, with 192 secret bits at the 95 % lower bound of the min-entropy (r = 3, t = 4, 1,395 cells), and its generated decoder of 8,984 GE matches the model bit for bit in 72 of 72 reconstructions. The designed parts of the complete security subsystem total 38,638 GE.
10. **A mixed-level simulation of the complete sensor** (Section XII). Extracted SRAM cells and post-layout StrongARM comparators inside the modulator loop run at transistor level together with the RTL key decoder and Grain-128a/MAC block; the key is regenerated from transistor-level cells, the comparator's decisions agree with the comparator model within its noise, and a recorded 30,000-clock replica attack recovers the original design's key from 8 of 80 windows and the proposal's from none.

The transmission result is stated with its limits, as bits, since no radio is designed in this work: at the oversampling ratio of 50 used for every accuracy result, the proposal sends **1.44–1.60× fewer bits** than the decimate-and-Trivium design of [1] (the 2.66× obtained at OSR 30, the setting of [1]'s headline 2.67×, does not hold at OSR 50), whereas an **information-equivalent conventional design**, which decimates on the sensor and encrypts 16-bit words at the Nyquist rate, sends **3.1× fewer bits than the proposal**.

## 1. Problem, proposal and approach

**The application.** Floating electrochemical sensors (Fig. 2) report the glucose concentration and the cell density inside bioreactors over an open radio link to the process control loop. Yasar *et al.* [1] protect such a sensor inside its delta-sigma ADC (the S-DSM): every 1-bit modulator output is XORed with a bit of a 16-bit LFSR keystream. Security costs 24 µW (1.8 % of the chip), and because the bits are sent without decimation the wireless energy is reported to be up to 2.67× lower than that of a design that decimates and then encrypts with Trivium.

**Wider applications.** The chip's three modes, cyclic voltammetry, amperometry and electrochemical impedance spectroscopy, underlie electrochemical sensing well beyond bioreactors (Section I-A):

* **medical monitoring:** blood-glucose meters and continuous glucose monitors (amperometric enzyme electrodes); lactate, oxygen and pH in blood and sweat; and neurotransmitters such as dopamine, measured by fast-scan cyclic voltammetry;
* **implants and wearables:** impedance monitoring of implanted electrodes and the surrounding tissue, and sweat-sensing patches;
* **diagnostics, food and water:** point-of-care biosensors for bacteria, viruses and proteins, and voltammetric detection of heavy metals and other contaminants;
* **energy and industry:** state-of-health estimation of batteries and fuel cells, and monitoring of corrosion and electroplating baths.

In medical use the readings are personal health data and can drive therapy, such as an insulin dose, so confidentiality and integrity matter even more there. The secure link studied here applies in principle to any such sensor whose current is digitised by a delta-sigma modulator and transmitted wirelessly.

**Research questions.** The evaluation in [1] covers an eavesdropper with a replica chip and two cryptanalyses; its key is stored, its keystream linear and its frames unauthenticated. The notebook asks four questions and answers them with open-source tools and the SkyWater SKY130 PDK:

1. **How secure is the S-DSM against a realistic attacker**, one who may also search the key space, learn a few keystream bits, obtain a chip or transmit on the link?
2. **Can the stored key be eliminated and every frame authenticated, without changing the analog front end, and at what cost?** The proposal regenerates a 128-bit key at every power-up from SRAM cells (an SRAM physically unclonable function, PUF) through a BCH fuzzy extractor and a hash, replaces the LFSR by Grain-128a, and authenticates every frame with Grain-128a's MAC, a frame counter and a session nonce.
3. **Do transistor-level circuits change these answers?** The two SKY130 circuits that set the analog behaviour, the modulator's StrongARM comparator and the 6T SRAM-PUF cell, are simulated from extracted layouts across process, voltage and temperature (PVT) and carried to the accuracy, power and security of the sensor.
4. **Can the key generator be derived from a specification, and do the models hold together?** A compiler turns the reliability and secrecy specification into a generated decoder, verified in RTL simulation and laid out, and one mixed-level simulation of the complete sensor tests the abstractions behind the system-level results.

## 2. Results and discussion

The results follow the order of the notebook: the model and its consistency check, the block level, the system level, security, cost, the compiler and the mixed-level simulation.

### 2.1 Consistency check against the measurements of [1] (Sections V and VI)

**Method.** The model's two-channel experiment reproduces Fig. 4(c) of [1]: two modulators encrypt the same input with the same key, and the fraction of bits that differ is measured for every combination of dither, first-integrator noise and inter-channel mismatch. The energy model reproduces the table of Fig. 4(b) of [1].

**Result.** With dither, noise and 0.10 % FS mismatch, 50.0 % (zero input) and 48.2 % (same message) of the bits differ, against 50.05 % and 49.8 % measured; the ratios of transmitted bits behind the energy table match the energy ratios of [1] within 0.18 % (Fig. 4). On the same message, the model's offset alone gives 48.2 %, thermal noise without offset 26.2 %, and the model's simplified dither alone 4.8 %.

**Discussion.** For independent channels the same-message value cannot exceed 48.4 % with the model's test sine, so the agreement is a consistency check rather than a validation. [1] attributes the disagreement to its dither. The model represents the dither in a simplified form, in which it changes only 4.8 % of the bits on its own, so the model reproduces the measured value with a small offset between the channels (from 0.01 % of full scale upwards); this is a limit of the model, not a finding about the chip. Analog differences between chips are therefore the central question for the security of the original design, which Section VIII answers.

**Result.** The comparator takes six noiseless decisions down to ±0.2 mV correctly (Fig. 6), and the extraction changes no quantity beyond its statistical error except the clock energy and a 0.1 mV systematic offset. Across the 45 PVT conditions (Fig. 9) the comparator spans σ<sub>n</sub> 0.37–0.81 mV, σ<sub>os</sub> 4.01–5.85 mV and 144–822 fJ per decision; temperature dominates its noise and offset, and its energy is dynamic (E/V<sub>DD</sub><sup>2</sup> 59–70 fF at 27 °C) except at 125 °C at the fast-nMOS corners, where leakage raises it to 210 fF. The SRAM cell's error rate rises on both sides of 25 °C (Fig. 7). Across corners the cell is stable at tt and ss, noisier at sf, and at ff and fs its errors at 85 °C are mostly reproducible flips (25.6 % of the cells at fs, 8.4 % at ff), which a closed-form error law explains as a rotation of the mismatch by up to 50° (Fig. 10). A recorded check with floating or grounded bit lines and without pass-gate mismatch leaves these flips unchanged within one cell in 128 and measures the rotation directly (53° at fs, against 58° from the error rate), and a supply-ramp sweep (0.1–100 µs) changes about 4 % of the cells against the 1 µs enrolment.

**Discussion.** The SKY130 values lower the PUF noise-to-mismatch ratio by a factor of 2.7 compared with the commonly assumed 0.06, but the temperature dependence is stronger than assumed, and the symmetric linear law s(T) = s<sub>25</sub>(1 + a|T − 25|) underestimates the 85 °C rate (1.75 % against 3.14 %). The code is therefore sized from the simulated error rates over 1,000 Monte Carlo chips of a two-component cell model (noise and mismatch rotation) fitted to the post-layout SPICE Monte Carlo of the cell: a key failure below 10<sup>−6</sup> from 0 to 85 °C needs t = 39 at the 99th percentile (33–46 over the 95 % interval of the 85 °C rate). Secrecy is bounded from below: with the 95 % lower bound on the min-entropy (0.908 bit per cell) and the 192 bits that a 128-bit key needs with a hash margin (ε = 2<sup>−32</sup>), at most t = 35 is admissible. **With 600 cells and a 25 °C enrolment neither requirement is met with margin**, so t = 45, which leaves 94–137 secret bits, is a working setting for the system simulations; the two-temperature enrolment (Section VIII-F) and the compiled codes (Section XI) size the key generator. Two further design rules follow from the block level: at the fast-nMOS corners temperature reverses the preference of cells rather than making them noisier, so a design verified at tt and 25 °C is not sufficient; and the key must be reconstructed with the supply ramp it was enrolled with.

### 2.3 System level: from transistor to system (Section VIII)

**Method.** The model of the complete sensor, with its SKY130 parameters, carries every circuit quantity to the system's own metrics: the SNDR of the decoded reading, the sensor's power, the success of the replica attack on the original design and the key-failure probability of the proposal. The replica attacker owns a second chip, XORs the ciphertext with that chip's bits and runs Berlekamp-Massey; the notebook judges security against a **full-scan attacker** who tries every 64-bit window of up to four frames, with independent seeds for every chip pair and 95 % Clopper–Pearson intervals, and counts a setting as protected only when none of at least 100 pairs is broken (the 80-window attacker of [1] is reported alongside). Each of the six studies first predicts its effect with a first-order theory (describing function of the 1-bit quantiser, offset as an initial-state shift, clean-window bound, leaky-integrator NTF, scale invariance in g<sub>2</sub>, the SRAM-PUF error law) and then simulates it at the 45 PVT conditions and far beyond them.

**The complete sensor, end to end.** One frame of the proposed design passes through every block with the SKY130 values, at the nominal condition and with every block at the sf corner and 85 °C, the condition of the noisiest comparator. The tag verifies, every bit is delivered, and the SNDR is 60.0 and 60.1 dB. The key is recovered at both (13 and 40 cell errors against t = 45), but at sf and 85 °C a power-up yields a wrong key with probability 0.13, which is why study F sizes the key generator per corner. The bit-stream spectrum (Fig. 11) has a white floor set by the integrator's thermal noise (−126.8 dBFS per bin from 2 to 8 kHz) and, above it, the 40 dB-per-decade shaping of the second-order loop; the in-band noise is −66.5 dBFS at both conditions, so the shaped quantisation noise, not the circuit noise, sets the SNDR. Assumed impairments that the reference model lacks (10 ps rms DAC jitter, 50 kHz 1/f corner, HD3 = −70 dBc) raise the low-frequency floor by 19 dB and lower the SNDR by 0.5 dB, so the 60 dB of the model is an upper bound for a chip.

**Result.**
* *Accuracy* (study A, Fig. 12). The SNDR is 60.3–60.4 dB at all 45 PVT conditions. The comparator noise would have to grow 88-fold (to about 72 mV) before 3 dB are lost; an offset of 3× the largest PVT σ<sub>os</sub> moves the decoded reading by 0.47 ppm of full scale. The describing-function theory places the −3 dB point within a factor of 1.4 with no fitted parameter.
* *Security of the original design* (studies B, D and E; Figs. 13 and 14). The full-scan attacker breaks 360 of 360 chip pairs at the PVT conditions: two chips disagree on 25 % of their bits, yet 4.9 % of the 64-bit windows remain clean, because the disagreements come in bursts. Comparator noise protects the design only from σ<sub>n</sub> = 160 mV, 197× its largest PVT value, at a cost of 7.5 dB of SNDR; the 80-window attacker of [1] already fails on most pairs from about 60 mV, so judging protection by that attacker would understate the required noise by more than 2×. With it, t = 17 meets 10<sup>−6</sup> for at least 99.7 % of the chips at every corner and leaves 284–352 secret bits at fs, with 935 cells for 600 kept cells.

**Discussion.** In the original design, the analog quantities that could make two chips differ enough to stop the attack do so only at a direct cost in accuracy, and the others never do: its security must come from a key. In the proposal, the only circuit that matters for security is the SRAM cell, and what it requires, a code and an enrolment whose two temperatures bracket the operating range, sized for the worst corner, is a system-level design decision rather than a circuit modification. Because the corners at risk cannot be identified at room temperature (fs has the lowest error rate of all corners at 25 °C), key reconstruction must also be tested hot. The comparator needs no design change; the integrators need an OTA gain of about 1.2× the oversampling ratio.

In the model, the proposal withstands all 15 (9 verdicts simulated, 2 analytic, 4 assumed) and detects and rejects five; without its chip nonce it would fall to A15 (correlation 0.95), with it the correlation is −0.01. The helper-data oracle accepted 0 of 200 tampered power-ups and answered every query exactly as a chip with a different fingerprint. The parameter sweeps show how thin the analog margins of the original design are against the attacker of [1] alone: the replica attack succeeds at 10 of 56 grid points (including 0.03 % FS replica offset with the dither of [1], one grid step below the assumed offset) and saturation at 21 of 81 (every point with buffer gain ≥ 1.0 and forced current ≥ 2 µA); the proposal is breached at none of the 137 points.

**Discussion.** The failures of the original design have three architectural causes: its key is short, linear and stored; its keystream restarts in every frame, so any repetition of message, forced input or session exposes it; and its frames carry neither authentication nor freshness. The analog attacks that [1] considered succeed once the attacker uses the capabilities of the threat model, as Section VIII found for the comparator and the integrators. The proposal's security rests entirely on digital blocks (the PUF key generator, Grain-128a, the MAC and the counter), whose cost Section X determines. Grain-128a has a published full-round fast correlation attack in stream-cipher mode [18] (2<sup>113.8</sup> data, 2<sup>115.4</sup> time): impractical, but below the nominal 2<sup>128</sup>.

### 2.5 Implementation cost (Section X)

**Method.** The Verilog of every design is verified against published test vectors at RTL and gate level (178 of 178 checks pass), in two forms: as written for one bit per clock, and in a low-power form given equally to every design (a radix-16 parallel state update, which updates the cipher state once every 16 clocks, and clock gating of every register in the cycles in which it is not written). Each is synthesised with Yosys on `sky130_fd_sc_hd`, placed with its clock tree with OpenROAD and simulated at gate level on its own test job (tt, 25 °C, 1.8 V, 10 MHz); its power comes from the switching activity of that simulation. The low-power block of the proposal is also routed in a recorded run (`ROUTE_BLOCK`), checked with Magic DRC, the antenna rules and netgen LVS, extracted with OpenRCX, timed at the slow, typical and fast corners, and re-simulated from the routed netlist. **No number is scaled from one process to another, and no modelled number enters the figure of merit.** The process enters only through a figure of merit, FOM = E<sub>bit</sub>/(L<sub>min</sub> V<sub>nom</sub><sup>2</sup> S K): the energy per encrypted bit E<sub>bit</sub> (measured for published works, gate-level simulated for this work's designed blocks) over the minimum gate length and nominal supply squared of the design's own process (486 nm·V² for SKY130, 93.6 nm·V² for the 65 nm process of [1]), per bit of security level S (the smaller of the key length and log<sub>2</sub> of the best published attack: S-DSM 16, Trivium 80, Grain-128a 113.8, AES-128 126.1), per security property K provided (of confidentiality, authentication, freshness, no key in NVM); in aJ/(nm·V²), lower is better. The nominal supply, not the operating supply, is used, so a design run below its nominal supply keeps that credit. No radio is designed, so no radio energy is claimed: the transmission is compared by the bits each architecture sends per reading at OSR 50.

**Result.** The low-power forms cut the proposal's power in SKY130 from 415 to 64.3 µW (6.5×), Trivium's from 223 to 32.3 µW (6.9×) and the S-DSM's from 43 to 18.1 µW (2.4×); the proposal falls from 9.7× to **3.6× the S-DSM** under the same conditions, a ratio that does not depend on the process. Of the 64.3 µW, the clock tree takes 32 %, the Grain-128a state 22 %, the MAC 18 % and the capture of the message bits for the MAC, which still runs at 10 MHz, 12 %; the gated key, nonce and counter registers draw almost nothing. The routed block (Fig. 18) is DRC and LVS clean and meets timing at three corners (setup +85.5 ns at ss, hold +0.12 ns at ff), its routed netlist passes its five gate-level checks, and it draws 73.4 µW with the extracted wires; 16 antenna violations remain, because this OpenROAD version repairs antennas only on the global routes. By the figure of merit (Fig. 19(b)), the low-power proposal reaches **29.1 aJ/(nm·V²)**, the lowest of all designs compared, against 1,603 for [1]'s measured S-DSM, 2,938 for its measured Trivium and 3,142 for the DTLS engine [9]; without the security credit it still takes 3.6× the normalised energy per bit of the S-DSM in the same flow. Placed unscaled on the chip of [1] (Fig. 20), its SKY130 power raises the chip from 1,376 to 1,416 µW: overhead (added power relative to the chip without its security block) 4.8 % against 1.8 % for the S-DSM and 16.3 % for Trivium, or 4.5 %, 1.7 % and 14.0 % of the chip's total.

**Discussion.** The FOM and the power ratio answer different questions: per unit of process and of security bought, the proposal is the most efficient design compared; in absolute power it costs 3.6× the S-DSM in the same flow, and part of its FOM advantage over [1]'s measured S-DSM comes from that block taking 2.9× the normalised energy per bit of its own RTL in this flow. The factor of 3.6 is the price of 256 bits of cipher state and 64 bits of MAC state against 16 bits of LFSR state per register, and of the parts that must run at the 10 MHz modulator clock; the process does not enter it. In transmitted bits the proposal keeps the position of the S-DSM: **1.44–1.60× fewer than the conventional design of [1]**, which sends 16-bit words at 4.5–5× the Nyquist rate. That advantage does not hold against an information-equivalent design: decimating on the sensor and encrypting 16-bit words at the Nyquist rate with Grain-128a + MAC sends **16.04 bits per reading, 3.1× fewer than the proposal**. What the bit-level architecture keeps is the absence of a decimator and a word-level datapath on the sensor, not the fewest bits per reading. No radio is designed in this work, so no radio energy is claimed.

### 2.6 Key-generator compiler (Section XI)

**Method.** The compiler `keygen.py` turns a specification (key failure below 10<sup>−6</sup> from 0 to 85 °C, process corners, enrolment, at least 128 secret bits) into hardware: it searches a concatenated code (an inner repetition code over r cells and an outer BCH code) with the corner models of Section VII-F, writes synthesisable Verilog for each candidate, verifies the generated decoder against the model in Icarus Verilog, synthesises the most promising candidates and places the selected one. Its detailed routing and Magic DRC are a recorded run (`ROUTE_COMPILED`). A SHA-256 core, verified against Python's `hashlib`, completes the area.

**Discussion.** The search turns the qualitative finding of Section VIII into the result of an exhaustive search under the cell models: **the two-temperature enrolment is what makes an all-corner specification achievable**, and its cleaner fingerprint needs so weak a code that the all-corner design is no larger than the typical-corner one. Without repetition the BCH-only designs would need t = 21–36 and, by the compiler's estimate, 1.5–2.2× the area. The size of the key generator, with the hash its largest part and a decoder close to the low-power keystream block, is a property of this work's round-per-clock SHA-256 and serial flip-flop decoder template, not of PUF key generation: a lightweight hash and a microcoded decoder, as in PUFKY [14], would be several times smaller. With t = 4, the success flag reliably reports a read beyond the code's capacity; a wrong key that escapes it would still be detected by the receiver when the first frame's MAC fails.

### 2.7 The complete sensor in one mixed-level simulation (Section XII)

**Method.** One simulation runs every block at the level at which its behaviour matters: the SRAM array as 640 extracted 6T cells in ngspice, the key decoder generated by the compiler and the Grain-128a/MAC block as RTL in Icarus Verilog, two chips' modulators with their post-layout StrongARM comparators inside the loop in ngspice, and the receiver and attackers in Python. Every decision of the transistor-level comparator is tested against the behavioural threshold model on the input it actually saw, with the number of disagreements that noise explains computed from theory. The live run covers 1,000 clocks at tt and 37 °C (a settings form chooses others); step 6, the replica attack over 30,000 clocks per chip, is a recorded run (`LONG_RUN`, 72 min on two cores).

**Result.** In the field read at 37 °C, 7 of the 600 transistor-level SRAM cells differ from the enrolment (1.17 %; 7.9 ± 2.8 expected from the recorded Monte Carlo); the generated RTL decoder corrects them, bit-identical to the model, and the server's key is recovered after 91,127 clock cycles (9.1 ms at 10 MHz). In the published run none of the 2,000 decisions of the two post-layout comparators differs from the threshold model (0.9 expected from their noise; 0 to 3 across the runs), and the transistor-level loop decodes to the same reading as the model (Fig. 22(b), (c), (f)). The RTL keystream block encrypts and authenticates exactly as the model, down to the 32-bit tag; the server accepts the frame, a frame with one changed bit is rejected, and an eavesdropper with a wrong key fails the MAC check and agrees with the true bits in 47.6 % of the clocks. The 1,000-clock replica attack is illustrative only (its 80 attempts fall on 23 distinct, overlapping windows). Over 30,000 clocks (recorded, Fig. 22(i)–(k)) the two chips disagree on 24.8 % of the bits and 5.1 % of the 64-bit windows are clean (behavioural model on the same pair: 3.9–4.6 % over 20 noise seeds); Berlekamp-Massey recovers the original design's key from 8 of 80 windows and the proposal's from none. On the frame itself (Fig. 22(l)–(n)), a search of all 65,535 keys of the original design with the replica's transistor-level bits as the plaintext guess ranks the true key first (91.2 % agreement against 50.0 ± 1.6 % for the wrong keys); against the proposal neither 1,000 random keys nor the 128 keys one bit from the true key do better than chance (50.1 ± 1.6 % and 49.8 ± 1.7 %); the receiver rejects all 1,080 single-bit forgeries of the RTL frame and 100,000 random tags, while the original design accepts every payload change; and the ciphertext passes four NIST SP 800-22 tests (p = 0.06 to 0.93) that the modulator bits fail (p ≤ 3 × 10⁻²⁶).

**Discussion.** The abstraction behind the system-level results holds where it could fail: in the closed loop, the post-layout transistor-level comparator shows no memory, kickback or metastability effect at this length (2,000 decisions cannot reveal an effect rarer than about one in a thousand), and the key generator works on transistor-level cells with its decoder simulated in RTL. The transistor-level pair is slightly burstier than the behavioural model, so the behavioural statistics of Section VIII slightly underestimate the windows available to the attacker, not the reverse. The replica result confirms, for one chip pair at one condition, that the analog differences between two chips do not protect the original design. The recorded long run predates the correction of the first-integrator noise (it uses twice the corrected value, which if anything makes the attack harder) and uses the 600-cell, t = 45 decoder rather than the compiled two-temperature design.

**What the comparison shows.** The proposal is the only approach in the table that provides every security property: crypto engines provide strong confidentiality but store their key and encrypt words after decimation, PUF key generators protect the key but do not secure a link, and the S-DSM keeps the cheapest link but fails 11 of the 15 attacks. By the FOM row it is the most efficient (29.1 against 1,603 aJ/(nm·V²) for the S-DSM and 3,142 for the DTLS engine); its price is power and area: 3.6× the S-DSM in the same flow, 4.5 % of [1]'s chip power with its unscaled SKY130 block, and 9,869 GE for the low-power keystream block (38,638 GE with the key decoder, hash and array), against 2,090 gates for NanoAES [8] and 548 for SIMON [10], which, however, still need a decimator and a stored key. In transmitted bits the proposal matches the S-DSM and sends fewer than the conventional design of [1], but more than an information-equivalent decimate-then-encrypt design; the table states both rows.

## 4. Conclusions, limitations and next steps

**Conclusions** (the four questions of Section I).
1. **In the model, the S-DSM is not secure against a realistic attacker.** With a simplified representation of the dither of [1], it withstands the cryptanalyses of [1] only as [1] carries them out, protected by the decorrelation between the victim's and the replica's bits; an attacker who scans the whole capture, trims the replica or votes over frames removes that protection, and in all it fails 11 of the 15 attacks.
2. **The stored key can be eliminated and every frame authenticated** without changing the front end: the PUF-keyed, authenticated design withstands all 15 attacks in the model (9 verdicts simulated, 2 analytic, 4 assumed). The cost is digital power (3.6× the S-DSM under strictly equal treatment; 4.5 % of [1]'s chip power with the unscaled SKY130 block; FOM 29.1 against 1,603 aJ/(nm·V²), the most efficient design compared) and area (9,869 GE for the low-power keystream block, 38,638 GE for the designed security subsystem). The transmitted bits stay at the S-DSM's level, 1.44–1.60× fewer than [1]'s conventional design at OSR 50, but 3.1× more than an information-equivalent design that decimates before encrypting; no radio energy is claimed.
3. **The simulated SKY130 circuits do not change the accuracy, and do not secure the original design**: 60.3–60.4 dB SNDR at all 45 PVT conditions, and no analog quantity stops the full-scan replica attacker without a direct loss of accuracy. **They do change the key**: with 600 cells enrolled at 25 °C neither the reliability target nor the 192-bit secret budget is met with margin, and at the fast-nMOS corners the mismatch rotates with temperature; the key generator must be enrolled at both ends of the temperature range (−40 and 85 °C: t = 17, 284–352 secret bits) and sized for its worst corner.
4. **A compiler derives a key generator that meets the target at all five corners**, verified bit for bit and routed, and **the abstractions hold** for one chip pair at one condition in a mixed-level simulation of the complete sensor with transistor-level SRAM cells and comparators, which also reproduces the replica attack on the original design over 30,000 clocks (recorded).

**Limitations.**
* **Simulation, not fabrication.** The circuit results are SKY130 simulations of extracted layouts; the comparison with silicon relies on the measurements reported in [1].
* **The SRAM noise is thermal only** (no 1/f, random-telegraph or supply noise, no ageing), so s<sub>25</sub> = 0.0225 is a lower bound; with the commonly assumed 0.06 a 600-cell design would need t = 60, which leaves no secret bits. Spatial correlation between cells is not modelled.
* **The SRAM-PUF corner analysis uses 512 cells and two held-out power-ups per corner**, which resolve error rates of about 0.1 %, and covers process × temperature (−40, 25 and 85 °C) at the nominal supply only; the supply-ramp effect is measured on 128 cells at one corner. The compiler's specification C uses the enrolment at 25 and 85 °C; Section VIII-F shows that −40 and 85 °C is needed to also remove the cells that flip in the cold.
* **The layouts are single cells drawn with the logic design rules**; the comparator's output buffers and SR latch remain schematic; the analog extraction is capacitance only. The modulator's integrators, TIA and buffer are behavioural (with thermal noise, saturation, OTA gain and capacitor mismatch as parameters).
* **Digital power is computed at the typical corner** with zero-delay activity (no glitches), and is not scaled to 65 nm. Timing covers three library corners without on-chip variation or IR drop. The key-reconstruction unit's detailed routing is a recorded run; the array periphery, the downlink receiver, the hash's padding and control and the power of the decoder and hash are not costed, and the blocks are not integrated into one layout.
* **The mixed-level simulation covers one chip pair at one condition** per run; its 30,000-clock replica run is recorded, with twice the corrected first-integrator noise and the t = 45 decoder.
* **The comparison with prior art crosses technology nodes** and favours the prior works.

**Next steps.** A transistor-level SKY130 integrator and TIA in the mixed-level simulation; an SRAM-PUF cell model with 1/f and random-telegraph noise and ageing, with the compiler re-run against it and dark-bit masking [15] as an alternative to the two-temperature enrolment; the compiled two-temperature key generator, with its array, in the mixed-level simulation at the fast-nMOS corners; the complete security subsystem (key-reconstruction unit, a lightweight hash, the MAC delay line and the keystream block) in one layout with the power of every block; and a tape-out in a node comparable to the prior works, where the power can be measured rather than simulated.
